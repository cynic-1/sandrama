# Storyboard Narrative Technique · Historical Epic · Technical Reference

---

## 1. Storyboard positioning

Storyboards are the core tool for directors to transform scripts into lens language. The form fields are set by the director according to the needs of the project (shot number, scene, camera movement, duration, characters, events, lines, light and shadow, emotions, transitions, etc.). The following only provides technical reference for the historical epic narrative type.

---

## 2. Scene selection

- **Visions are the breath of the epic** — Visions/grand vistas should be used more frequently in epic narratives than in other genres. The vision conveys "the vastness of the times and the insignificance of individuals" and is the core source of the epic feeling.
- **Close-ups are human anchors** — After each grand vision paragraph, arrange at least one close-up of a character as an emotional anchor. The scene-jumping "from heaven and earth to eyes" is a signature technique of epic narrative
- **The mid-shot carries the group drama** — A large number of multi-person conversations, court confrontations, and military tent discussions in the epic require the mid-shot to explain the character relationships and spatial positions.
- **Large close-ups are reserved for moments of fate** — Large close-ups (eyes/hands/tokens) are only used for moments of "turning of fate": the hand receiving the imperial edict, the look in the eyes before making a decision, the broken token
- **The establishing shot must have an epic feel** — The establishing shot not only explains the space, but also conveys "what kind of era this is" - a desolate pass has more narrative power than a subtitle labeled "xx city"

---

## 3. Rhythm of camera movement

- **Slow by Default** — The camera speed in epic narratives should be slowed down across the board, with more than 70% of the shots being static or extremely slow motion. Rapid movement of the camera is incompatible with the calm temperament of the epic
- **Slow Push = Destiny Focus** — "The eyes of history are focused on this person/this matter", suitable for key decisions and heroic moments
- **Slow pull = reveal the whole picture** — Slowly pull out from the individual to reveal the grand panorama (battlefield/ruins/crowd of people), which is a classic epic mirror of "from personal destiny to the torrent of the times"
- **Overlook Panning = Picture Scroll of the Times** — Slowly panning at a high angle to show big scenes, like unfolding a historical picture.
- **Steady gaze = Historical witness** — The footage of key historical moments should be absolutely still, like a witness staring. Stillness itself is the most solemn "mirror movement"

---

## 4. Duration control

- **Vision/Grand Vision 5-7s** — Epic vistas need enough time for the audience to feel the vastness of the space and the weight of history. Don’t rush to cut away.
- **Confrontation/Conversation Close Up 4-5s** — Dialogue in epics is slower paced than in daily types. Leave a silent space between each sentence to give the language the weight of "grounding"
- **Lengthened Ritual Paragraphs** — Each action in a ritual scene (kneeling/oathing/sacrifice) should be given a sufficient length of time (3-5s/action). The solemnity of the ceremony comes from "not rushing"
- **Close-up 3-4s** — Close-ups in epics carry "moments of destiny" and require a longer stay than usual to allow the audience to feel the emotional weight
- **Battle scenes will not be shortened** — Although the pace of battle scenes is accelerated, the duration of a single shot should not be greatly compressed. Create tension by switching scenes rather than shortening the duration
- **Golden rule: solemnity comes from lack of haste** — All rhythms in epic narratives should be "half a beat slower" than ordinary ones. Haste = frivolity, leisurely = solemnity

---

## 5. Lens merging strategy (removing the sense of AI)

- **Group scenes covered in one shot** - Multi-person scenes such as court halls, military tents, banquets, etc. can be explained with one lens with a moving lens (slowly moving across the group portrait) without breaking it into individual characters.
- **March/Migration Merge** — Moving scenes such as marching columns and people migrating should be merged into one continuous moving shot. Shredding will destroy the "flood feeling"
- **Narrative Density Priority** — Each shot must advance the narrative or convey epic emotions. Purely expositional environmental shots should be merged into shots with narrative functions.
- **Director's Thought Test** — Self-check after writing the storyboard: If an epic film director will use a slow long shot to complete the sequence, don't break it into pieces.

---

## 6. One shot to the end (long shot merge)

- **Applicable Conditions** — Battlefield crossing, the entire ceremony, and characters traveling through space from one state to another. The value of long shots in epic narratives is to "let the audience be with history"
- **Typical Scenes** — The character walks through the ruins after the war, the whole process of the army's departure, the ceremonial march from the palace gate to the main hall, and the journey from one end to the other on the battlefield.
- **Marking method** - Write the complete path in the movement field (such as "One shot to the end: overlooking the army starting → Slowly push down to the side of the protagonist → Follow and move parallel to the protagonist → Fall and the protagonist looks back at the city gate"), explain the starting and ending angles in the picture description
- **Duration Relaxation** — Epic long shots can exceed the 6s upper limit of a single shot due to the continuous updating of information and the need for immersion, but not exceed 15s
- **Gravity first** — The movement speed of long shots should always be solemn and slow, and there should be no rush even in battle scenes.

---

## 7. Character interaction design

- **Confrontation is the home ground of the epic** — The most tense interaction in the epic narrative is not the fight, but the confrontation: the silence between the two people looking at each other, the undercurrent on the negotiation table, and the temptation between the monarch and his ministers.
- **Individuals in group portraits** — There should be a "focus character" whose emotional line runs through the group interaction. The group is the background, and individual reactions are the narrative anchor.
- **Body language is power** — Character interactions in the epic highly rely on body language to convey power relationships: standing and kneeling, front and back, high and low, facing and facing away

---

## 8. Lines and blank spaces

- **Epic lines should be short** — A punchy line is more powerful than a speech. Keep key dialogue within one sentence, giving the language the weight of being "engraved in stone"
- **Silence is the punctuation of an epic** — There must be enough silence before and after key lines. Silence allows the audience to "hear" the weight of the lines
- **Use narration with caution** — Epic narratives should try not to use narration to explain the historical background. Using images, space, and character reactions to convey information is more convincing than narration.

---

## 9. Transition design

- **Empty mirror transition for scene switching** — Insert an epic empty mirror (flag, city wall, sky, wasteland) between different scenes as an emotional buffer, while conveying the texture of the times.
- **Transition of time span using images** — To express the passage of time, do not use date subtitles, but use images to dissolve: new snow covers the old battlefield, seedlings grow into big trees, and new people take the place of the old ones.
- **Fade to black/fade to bright between paragraphs** — Use a slow fade to black to fade to bright transition between large paragraphs to simulate the ritual feeling of "turning the page of history"
- **Default Hard Cut** — Use hard cuts between shots in the same scene to keep the narrative clean and solemn.
