# Narrative Technique · Science Fiction Apocalypse · Technical Reference

---

## 1. Theme conception and eschatological core

### Key points of science fiction apocalyptic narrative

- **Survival is the underlying operating system of narrative** — All behaviors in the apocalypse (walking, drinking, sleeping, trusting) are redefined by the premise of "survive". Every decision in the narrative should have a survival cost consideration
- **The wreckage of civilization is narrative** — The remaining traces of civilization in the ruins (a family photo, a rusty amusement ride, an abandoned school bus) have their own narrative power - they silently tell "what used to be here". Make good use of environmental narratives instead of dialogue explanations
- **Humanity is revealed in extremes** — The end of the world is a natural "laboratory of humanity": who will be abandoned first when resources are insufficient? Who chooses to sacrifice in a life-and-death situation? Who would waver when betrayal can buy a way out? Extreme environments amplify the human nature that is usually hidden until there is nowhere to hide it
- **Suggested directions for leaving the scene** — Desolation and shock / Survival from desperate situation / Human torture / Bitter hope. Avoid "relaxed", "humorous", "sweet" and other directions that do not match the apocalyptic temperament
- **The weight of hope is directly proportional to despair** — Hope in the apocalypse cannot be cheap. The darker the world, the more precious a ray of light is - a green bud growing out of the ruins, someone extending a hand in despair, the distant voice heard when one is about to die. Hope must be based on despair

## 2. Narrative structure and rhythm of survival

### Key points of science fiction apocalyptic narrative

- **Crisis Driven Narrative** — The engine of the apocalyptic narrative is not "what you want" but "you must survive today." Each paragraph should have a clear survival crisis (resources exhausted, threats approaching, shelter lost, companions injured). Crisis drives action, and action drives narrative.
- **Resources are Conflict** — Food, water, medicine, ammunition, safe habitat — resource scarcity is the source of apocalyptic conflict. The audience should always feel the urgency of "not enough" in the narrative
- **Progressive Test of Trust** — Trust is more scarce than resources in the apocalypse. The line of trust follows the progression of "test → cooperation → test → betrayal or persistence". The stakes of each trust test should be higher than the previous one
- **Use the wasteland to breathe between paragraphs** — Use low-intensity paragraphs such as walking in the wilderness/exploring ruins between crisis paragraphs as an emotional buffer. These passages are not "empty" but supplement the worldview with environmental narratives
- **Hope Curve** — The emotion of the apocalyptic narrative is not a continuous decline, but a jagged curve of "despair → glimmer → greater despair → stronger glimmer". Give a glimmer of hope every time you hit bottom (find water, meet friendly people, find shelter), and then overthrow the hope with a bigger blow
- **The ending does not have to be happy** — The best ending of an apocalyptic narrative is not "everything returns to normal" but "carrying on with the scars". Survival itself is victory, there is no need to save the world

## 3. Scene atmosphere design

### Key points of science fiction apocalyptic narrative

- **The atmosphere is described in terms of decay** — don't say "desolate," say "rust spreads to the third step, and weeds squeeze out of the cracked floor tiles." Concrete details of decay have more impact than abstract words
- **Typical apocalyptic passages and designs** —

| Paragraph Types | Emotional Direction | Narrative Techniques | Music Suggestions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exploration of ruins | Alertness + Curiosity | Slowly advance, scan the space, and discover the remains of civilization | Low-frequency buzzing + occasional metal collision sounds |
| Struggle for resources | Urgency + Confrontation | Search for supplies, compete with others, make trade-offs | Rhythmic background noise crescendo |
| Respite/Camp | Tired + Vulnerable | Around the fire, simple food sharing, short-term human warmth | Very low volume guitar or harmonica solo |
| Crisis/Threat | Fear + Survival | Being chased, shelter lost, companions injured | Heartbeat drumbeat + piercing alarm or total silence |
| Human choice | Struggle + heaviness | Whether to abandon the wounded, whether to open the last can | The soundtrack disappears and pure silence |
| Glimmer of hope | Bitterness + warmth | Green buds found in the ruins, distant voices, sunrise | Single instrumental music fades from silence to warmth |

- **Space decay is time narrative** — The degree of environmental decay directly narrates "how long has it been since the end of the world":
  - **Near-term apocalypse**: The building is basically intact but empty, and items are scattered as if they had just left.
  - **Mid-Term Doom**: Rusted, overgrown, partially collapsed, nature begins to take back territory
  - **Long-term doomsday**: ruins, ruins, and vegetation cover everything, leaving only the outline of civilization traces
- **Environment is the Threat** — In the apocalypse, the environment itself is the enemy: toxic air, unstable buildings, polluted water sources, extreme weather. Storyboards should always make the audience feel the hostility of the environment
- **The purpose of the shot is to write "feelings of survival" rather than "scene description"** - "Using long shots is to make the audience feel the insignificance and helplessness of people in the boundless ruins" is better than "using long shots to shoot ruins"

## 4. Sound and Music Direction

### Key points of science fiction apocalyptic narrative

- **Silence is the background of the apocalypse** — The most disturbing thing about the apocalypse is not the roar of monsters, but "the lack of sound where there should be sound": deserted cities, factories that are no longer functioning, and silent playgrounds. Silence itself narrates "death"
- **Industrial waste noise** — Metal friction, leaking pipes, the sizzling sound of wire short circuits, and unknown roars in the distance - these "sounds of the remains of civilization" constitute a unique soundscape of the end of the world
- **The soundtrack is extremely restrained** — The soundtrack coverage of the entire film does not exceed 30%. The power of the apocalypse lies in the "sense of vacuum". Too much soundtrack will weaken the impact of desolation.
- **Narrative function of alarm/signal tone** — The sounds occasionally emitted by the remaining electronic equipment (no signal broadcast, looped emergency broadcast, intermittent buzzer) are the unique "ghost sounds" of the end of the world, which are very atmospheric.
- **Music Emotion Model** —

| Emotional Stages | Music Strategy | Coverage |
|---|---|---|
| Ruins Exploration | Low-frequency hum + occasional metallic sound | Low |
| Daily survival | No soundtrack, pure ambient sound | None |
| Crisis is approaching | Heartbeat drumbeat, crescendo | Medium |
| Human Choice | Complete Silence | None |
| Fight/flight | Rhythmic electronic noise + percussion | Medium-high |
| Glimmer of Hope | Single warm instrumental music eases in | Low → Medium |

- **A Certain Atmosphere** — A single sudden sound in the apocalypse has more impact than a rich soundscape: a distant gunshot, the creak of a door blown open by the wind, a cry for help from the rubble. In total silence, a sound is an event

## 5. Composition and scenery narrative

### Key points of science fiction apocalyptic narrative

- **Narrative function of three core compositions** —
  - **Panoramic view of ruins** — Tiny human figures are embedded in large areas of ruins, conveying the apocalyptic feeling that "civilization has passed and people are like ants". It is the most frequently used composition in science fiction apocalypse.
  - **Oppressed Space** — Walking through low ceilings/narrow passages/collapsed ruins, the oppression of physical space directly conveys the suffocation of survival.
  - **Contrast between people and ruins** — Juxtaposing people with the ruins of civilization (people standing in front of abandoned skyscrapers/walking on abandoned highways), using scale contrast to narrate the fall of civilization
- **The "last man" feeling in the central composition** — The central composition implies the extreme loneliness of "you are the only one left in this ruins" in the apocalypse, leaving the iconic shot of a lone walker in the ruins.
- **The sense of disorder in the tilted composition** — The Dutch angle conveys the psychological suggestion that "the world has tilted/disordered" in the apocalypse, which is suitable for passages in the early stages of disaster, mental breakdown, and extremely dangerous environments.
- **Foreground ruins blocking** — Use the ruins as the foreground blocking subject to create a sense of peeping/searching of "seeing survivors through the ruins"
- **Scenery is the state of survival** —
  - **Grand Vision/Vision** — The overall view of ruins, endless wasteland, and the macroscopic shock of the demise of civilization
  - **Panorama** — The position and relationship of survivors in space, and the direction of threats
  - **Medium Shot** — Survival behavior (search, alert, walking), character interaction
  - **Close-up** — Survival details (chapped lips, dusty hands, last can of food), emotional focus
- **The "relics" function of close-ups** — Close-ups often focus on "relics of civilization" in the apocalypse: a faded family photo, a dead mobile phone screen, a doll covered in gray. These details carry a thousand-word narrative

## 6. Camera movement and rhythm

### Key points of science fiction apocalyptic narrative

- **Handheld is the basic language of the apocalypse** — More than 50% of the shots are slightly handheld, conveying the existential anxiety of "unstable/accidents may happen at any time". Grading of shaking amplitude: slight shaking when safe → obvious shaking when dangerous → severe shaking during combat
- **Slow Movement Exploration** — The ruins exploration section uses extremely slow horizontal movement, and the simulated character advances carefully. The slower the speed, the more nervous the audience is ("What will we see next?")
- **Quick push = Crisis breaks out** — Push quickly when a threat suddenly appears, simulating the physiological reaction of "being frightened/shocked"
- **Scale = Reveal Desolation** — Slows out from characters/details to distant views, revealing the immensity of the surrounding ruins. It is the most classic "sighing mirror" in apocalyptic narratives
- **Segments without stabilizers** — The escape/battle segments deliberately do not use stabilizers, using real picture jitter to convey chaos and fear.
- **Moving the camera is the state of survival** — still = safety/alert; slow movement = exploration/search; handheld tracking = marching/restlessness; fast push = crisis/discovery; slow pull = despair/loneliness
- **360° Look Around the Ruins** — When the character arrives at a new location, he uses a slow 360° look around, allowing the audience and the character to "scan" the surrounding environment together to determine the safety. It has both setting and suspense functions.
