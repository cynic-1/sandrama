# Storyboard narrative techniques · Horror and supernatural · Technical reference

---

## 1. Storyboard positioning

Storyboards are the core tool for directors to transform scripts into lens language. The form fields are set by the director according to the needs of the project (storyboard numbers, scenes, camera movements, duration, characters, events, lines, light and shadow, emotions, transitions, etc.). The following only provides technical reference for the horror and supernatural narrative genre.

---

## 2. Scene selection

- **Scenery means information control** — The core logic of scene selection in horror narratives is to "control how much the audience knows." Close-up = the character's view is limited (the audience doesn't know what's around them); long shot = the character is exposed to the unknown (the audience sees the danger but the character doesn't)
- **The fear of closure in close-ups** — Close-ups/extreme close-ups make everything outside the screen become an "unknown area", and the audience will instinctively be anxious about "what is outside the screen". Fear accumulation paragraphs often use close-ups to limit information.
- **Fear of Exposure in Long Shots** — Long shots make characters appear small, isolated, and exposed, which is suitable for passages where "the threat is already nearby but the character does not know it"
- **Avoid the sense of security in the mid-shot** — The mid-shot is the "safest" shot (moderate information, moderate distance). The use of mid-shot should be reduced in horror narratives, and extreme switching between close-ups and long shots should be used to create uneasiness.
- **Establishing shots should create uneasiness** — Establishing the scene is not just about explaining the environment, but also burying "something wrong" in the establishing shots - blurry shapes at the edge of the screen, windows that shouldn't be bright, and spaces that are too quiet.

---

## 3. Rhythm of camera movement

- **Default is static, static means oppression** — More than 70% of the shots should be static. Stillness in horror narratives does not mean "no movement of the camera", but actively creates the suffocating feeling of "being trapped in this perspective"
- **Extremely slow push = approach** — Half the speed of normal push, implying "something is getting closer" or "you have to see this thing clearly"
- **Slow pull = Reveal the full horror picture** — Slowly pull away from the character or part to reveal the horrific truth that the character has not seen (there is someone behind you, the room is destroyed, everything is abnormal around you)
- **Sudden Stop** — The moving camera suddenly stopped, suggesting "something stopped everything", a strong psychological impact
- **Slight Breathing Jitter** — Extremely subtle fluctuations in the picture, simulating the presence of a "peeper" from a subjective perspective. It's not shaking hands, it's the audience's subconscious perception that "someone is watching"

---

## 4. Duration control

- **Fear Shot Stretch** — Uneasy/fear sequences should be 50% longer than normal. The audience is "forced" to stay on the disturbing picture and wait for possible horrific events.
- **Give enough time for silent shots** — Silent shots (ambient sound vacuum) for at least 3-4 seconds, so that the oppressive feeling of silence can fully affect the audience
- **The scare moment is extremely short** — Jump Scare shots are controlled within 1-1.5s, and they will be cut away before you can react, leaving a frightening aftermath.
- **Close-up stay 3-4s** — Close-ups of fearful expressions need to stay longer than normal to allow the audience to "infect" the character's fear
- **Long Shots/Empty Shots 5-7s** — Long shots and empty shots of horror atmosphere take longer to give the audience time to "search" for anomalies in the screen
- **Golden Rule: Fear comes from waiting** — The audience knows something terrible is going to happen but doesn’t know when it will happen. Every second of waiting amplifies fear.

---

## 5. Lens merging strategy (removing the sense of AI)

- **Continuous uneasiness cannot be cut off** — Shots that continuously create an uneasy atmosphere should be combined into long shots as much as possible. Once cut off (hard cut to a new angle), the accumulation of uneasiness will be reset.
- **Environment exploration is completed in one shot** — When a character explores an unknown space (entering a dark room, walking through a long corridor), one shot should be used to the end as much as possible. Shredding will destroy the immersive fear of "experience with the character"
- **Narrative Density Priority** — Every shot must advance fear or narrative, purely decorative atmospheric shots should be merged into shots with narrative function
- **Director's Thought Test** — Self-examination after writing the storyboard: Is the horror atmosphere continuous or interrupted by fragments? If a director uses long takes to complete a sequence, don't break it into pieces.

---

## 6. One shot to the end (long shot merge)

- **Applicable Conditions** — The character explores unknown space, chases/escapes, and gradually changes from safety to fear. The value of long shots in horror narratives is "not giving the audience a chance to breathe"
- **Typical Scenario** — The character walks alone through a dark corridor, opens a door to explore a room, enters an abnormal space from a normal space, and chases and escapes
- **Annotation method** - Write the complete path in the camera movement field (such as "One shot to the end: Follow the moving character through the corridor → Slowly push into the room → The back of the falling character is still"), explain the starting and ending angle in the picture description
- **Relaxation of duration** — Horror long lenses can exceed the 6s upper limit of a single lens due to the continuous creation of a sense of oppression, but shall not exceed 15s.
- **Suffocation Control** — The longer the shot takes, the stronger the audience's anxiety of "waiting for horror to happen". The long lens itself is a fear maker

---

## 7. Character interaction design

- **Solo shows are the home of horror** — The most effective passages in horror narratives are often when characters are alone. The proportion of single shots in the storyboard should be higher than that of group shots
- **The fear reaction is more important than the source of the fear** — It’s not what the character sees that matters, it’s how the character reacts that matters. The storyboard should prioritize shots of "characters' fearful reactions" rather than shots of "the horror itself"
- **Motion Restraint** — A person in fear will not make large movements, but will freeze/shrink/back away slowly. In the storyboard, avoid writing "the character screamed and ran away" and write "the character froze and clenched his fingers unconsciously"

---

## 8. Lines and blank spaces

- **Speak less, fear more** — Horror passages should have very few lines. The more the character talks, the more "sense of control" he or she feels. It is silence that allows fear to spread.
- **Fragmented lines** — Lines in fear should be fragmented: half a sentence, a name that cannot be spoken, and a low "don't move". Complete sentences will destroy the atmosphere of fear
- **The audience knows the character but not** — The best horror blank is "the audience has seen it but the character is still talking normally", using the character's ignorance to contrast the horror

---

## 9. Transition design

- **Default Hard Cut** — Use hard cuts between shots in the same scene to keep the tension clean and neat.
- **No buffering between horror paragraphs** — Unlike other genres, transitions between paragraphs in horror narratives should try not to give the audience a "rest" space, unless it is a deliberately arranged "fake safety"
- **The fear of black screen** — A short black screen (0.5-1s) can be used as a transition in horror narratives. During the black screen, the audience cannot see anything = the greatest fear. But don't abuse
- **Sound first transition** — The picture is still in the previous scene, but the sound is already the abnormal sound effect of the next scene. Use sound to "invade" the picture and create a sense of penetration of fear
