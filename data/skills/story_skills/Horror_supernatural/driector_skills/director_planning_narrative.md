# Narrative Techniques · Horror and Supernatural · Technical Reference

---

## 1. Theme conception and emotional core

### Key points of horror and supernatural narratives

- **The unknown is fear** — The real terror comes from "the unseen." Don't rush to reveal the whole monster/ghost, the audience's imagination is far scarier than any design. The theme should be centered around "unknown threats" rather than "known monsters"
- **Cracks in Everyday Life** — The most effective horror is the "off" details that appear in familiar daily routines: the clock goes backwards, there is an extra chair, there is an extra person in the photo. The idea should emphasize the "disintegration of security"
- **Slow Erosion** — Horror is not a one-time scare, but a continuous accumulation of uneasiness. The theme design should have an eroding arc of "from subtle to out of control", allowing the audience to slide from "something doesn't seem right" to "completely inescapable"
- **Suggested directions for leaving the scene** — Afraid/chills down the spine/fearful to think about/didn’t dare to look back. Avoid purely sensory stimulation directions such as "disgusting" and "bloody"
- **The human heart is scarier than ghosts** — The most advanced horror narrative, the supernatural elements are just a metaphor for the dark side of human nature. The real source of fear should point to the human heart: greed, suspicion, betrayal, madness

## 2. Narrative structure and rhythm planning

### Key points of horror and supernatural narratives

- **The accumulation of uneasiness is a basic skill** — The core task of the first half of the horror narrative is "accumulation of uneasiness". Use small anomalies in daily scenes to erode the audience's sense of security little by little. Each paragraph should be more "wrong" than the previous one.
- **Alternating between tight and loose to create a psychological gap** - Don’t be tight all the time, otherwise the audience will be numb. Insert "false safety" between scary passages (the character thinks the danger is over), allowing the audience to breathe a sigh of relief - and then tighten it again
- **Information control is the core engine** — The essence of horror is information asymmetry: the audience does not know what is there, and the characters do not know that danger is approaching. Narrative planning should clarify "what the audience knows" and "what the characters know" in each paragraph
- **Delayed Reveal** — Key horror elements (the true identity of the monster, the truth, the source of the threat) should be revealed as late as possible. Reveal a little bit at a time, creating a spiral of "the more you know, the more you fear"
- **Climax is the confirmation of fear** — All the uneasiness accumulated in the past is confirmed in the climax paragraph by "the worst premonition comes true". The climax is not the scariest moment, but the terrifying epiphany of "it was always..."
- **Virtual and Real Fuzzy Model** — The advanced technique of horror narrative is to make the audience unable to distinguish "whether it is real or the character's hallucination": reality → abnormality → doubt → confirmation → greater doubt. The more blurred the line between true and false, the stronger the sense of unease.

## 3. Scene emotional design

### Key points of horror and supernatural narratives

- **Use somatosensory words for emotional targets** — Instead of saying "fear", say "sudden chills on the back" and "want to turn my head but dare not". Emotional description at the somatosensory level can better guide narrative rhythm and performance design
- **Typical emotional passages and designs** —

| Paragraph type | Emotional direction | Narrative techniques | Sound effect suggestions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Daily establishment | Safety + warmth | Normal life scenes, deliberately creating "everything is fine" | Natural ambient sounds, relaxing atmosphere |
| Subtle anomaly | Faintly uneasy | A detail that shouldn't appear, the character didn't notice it but the audience saw it | An uncoordinated sound was mixed into the ambient sound |
| The sense of security disintegrates | Fear sprouts | The abnormal frequency increases, and the character begins to notice that "something is wrong" | The ambient sound gradually becomes thinner, and the low frequency gradually enters |
| Fake safety | Brief relaxation | The crisis seems to be over, the character breathes a sigh of relief | Normal ambient sound is restored, the music stops |
| Confirmation of fear | Extreme fear | Worst guesses confirmed, no escape | Sudden silence, or extremely low frequency oppression |
| Aftertaste/scare | Horrible if you think about it | It seems to be over but there is one "wrong" detail left behind | The previous abnormal sound is buried in the daily environmental sound |

- **"Security deprivation" is a core narrative tool** — using the character's safe zone to be gradually eroded to map the escalation of fear:
  - **Initial**: The threat is far away, outside, in the dark
  - **Midterm**: Threats enter the living space, but can still be evaded
  - **Late stage**: The threat is around/behind/in the body, there is nowhere to escape
- **Space is psychology** — Make good use of spatial features to convey a sense of fear. Closed space = nowhere to escape; open space = nowhere to hide; alienation in familiar space = collapse of sense of security
- **The "Ineffability" of Fear** — The most terrifying things should not be fully described. The character's reaction (dilated pupils, speechless, turning around and running away) is more effective than directly showing "that thing"

## 4. Sound and Music Direction

### Key points of horror and supernatural narratives

- **Silence is the most powerful tool of fear** — The killer feature of horror movies is not the screaming sound effect, but the absolute silence before it. Key horror passages should be designed with a "sound vacuum" - all ambient sounds suddenly disappear, and the audience instinctively holds their breath
- **Low-frequency oppression** — Low-frequency sounds (18-20Hz range) that are inaudible to the human ear but perceptible to the body can create physiological uneasiness. The use of low-frequency background noise in passages that build up uneasiness makes the audience feel uncomfortable even though they can’t explain why.
- **Unknown Source** — The golden rule of horror sound effects is "you hear it but don't know what's coming from it." Footsteps, breathing, scratching sounds - the more everyday sounds appear when they shouldn't, the scarier they are.
- **Environmental sound is a fear thermometer** - Use the changes in the level of environmental sound to mark the level of fear: normal (birdsong/insects/human voices) → thin (sound gradually disappears) → dead silence → abnormal sound appears
- **Music Emotion Progressive Model** —

| Emotional Stages | Music Strategy | Coverage |
|---|---|---|
| Daily safety | Normal soundtrack or pure ambient sound | Low |
| Uneasy buds | Dissonance intervals, extremely low volume | Mid-low |
| Fear escalates | Low-frequency background noise gradually intensifies, hinting at rhythm | Medium |
| Horror climax | Sudden silence or extreme sound effects | Extreme |
| False safety | Restore normal ambient sound | Low |
| Afraid of the aftertaste | Abnormal elements mixed into everyday sounds | Mid-low |

- **Restraint on "jump scares"** - Sudden loud noises (Jump Scare) can only be used 1-2 times at most, and must be used after a long period of silence. The abuse of jump scares is the lowest form of horror and will dispel the previously accumulated atmospheric fear.

## 5. Composition and scenery narrative

### Key points of horror and supernatural narratives

- **Narrative function of three core compositions** —
  - **Large area of blank space** — open area = potential threat area, the audience will instinctively "look" for sources of fear in the blank space
  - **Frame blocking composition** — Door gap/window/end of corridor, restricted view = restricted information = amplified fear
  - **Unbalanced composition** - the subject is tilted to one side of the frame, leaving "unsafe space" on the other side, suggesting that something is there
- **Limited vision is fear** — In horror narratives, the less the audience sees, the more frightened they are. Use scene and composition to control the amount of information: the audience cannot see behind the characters, the corners of the room, or the whole picture in the dark
- **The sense of threat in the depth of space** — Use the depth of space to create hints of "something in the distance": blurred outlines at the end of the corridor, things that shouldn't move in the background move, abnormal shapes out of focus
- **Scenery and Information Control** — Close-up = the character is unaware of what is going on around him (and neither is the audience); long shot = the character is exposed to a threat (the audience sees it but the character does not know it). Use scene switching to create different types of fear
- **The fear function of close-ups** — Close-ups (pupils/lips/fingers) are used in horror narratives to capture the "physiological response of fear": dilated pupils, trembling lips, nails digging into palms
- **"Extra things"** - The core technique of horror composition: elements that should not appear in the picture (a second face in the mirror, a silhouette in the shadow, an extra figure in the background), the audience's fear when they discover it is far greater than any sound effect

## 6. Camera movement and rhythm

### Key points of horror and supernatural narratives

- **Stillness = Oppression** — More than 70% of the shots in a horror narrative should be static. The still picture prevents the audience from gaining a sense of security that "someone is in control" through camera movements, creating the fear of loneliness of being "abandoned at the scene"
- **Extremely slow push = approaching/inescapable** — A slower push than normal push, suggesting "something is approaching" or "you have to face it", suitable for horror confirmation paragraphs
- **Slow zoom = reveal/discover the whole picture** — Slowly zoom out to reveal the full horror that the character has not seen (the figure behind him, the abnormality in the room), which is a classic technique of "the audience knows but the character does not know"
- **Sudden stillness** — The moving camera suddenly stops, implying that "something has stopped the camera", creating a strong sense of uneasiness.
- **Breathing Mirror Movement** — A slight jitter/undulation, simulating the subjective voyeuristic feeling of "someone is peeking here". It's not shaking with hands, it's a very subtle "breathing"
- **Screen movement indicates direction of threat** — The direction of camera movement indicates the source of the threat. The audience instinctively thinks that the direction of the camera movement is the direction of "what to see" - this expectation can be exploited, or it can be violated to create shock.
